Guide

How to get a fully funded PhD in the USA

This is not just my own story. After I started sharing my PhD journey on Instagram, many of you sent me questions, from “where do I start?” to “what does this professor’s reply mean?” I went through all of those messages and comments, about 390 questions from 272 conversations, which came down to 153 different questions. I grouped them by topic, answered them with my own experience, and checked the facts with official university sources. So you can see every part of getting a funded PhD in the USA in one place, and I will keep updating this page as new questions come in.

Gokul Dhanapati
PhD student in Entomology, Mississippi State University. About me

How a funded PhD in the USA works

You don’t pay; you get paid

Most PhD programs in the US are funded. The university waives your tuition fee, and you receive a monthly stipend. In return, you work part-time as a research assistant on a professor’s project, or as a teaching assistant. So you are not paying to study. You are paid to do research.

Getting a position comes first, funding comes with it

If a professor or a department accepts you as a PhD student, funding usually comes with that offer. Professors normally don’t accept PhD students unless they can fund them. So your main goal is to get accepted. Funding follows.

How a PhD is different from a master’s

A master’s in the US is mostly coursework (classes), and most master’s students pay their own fees. A PhD is mainly research, and is usually funded. Funded master’s positions also exist and you find them the same way, but there are far fewer. Some labs advertise “MS or PhD” and decide which one to offer based on your profile. Some even upgrade a master’s student to a PhD later.

How long it takes

It depends on the field, your project and whether you already have a master’s. Across all fields in the US, the median is about 5.7 years from the start of the doctoral program (engineering is a little shorter). Some students finish sooner; my project is funded for 3 years. You also take classes during a US PhD. Courses from an Indian master’s usually don’t count, or only a few credits transfer, so expect to take courses again.

Why I chose the USA

The US is one of the two biggest research spenders in the world (with China), and a large share of that research happens in universities, which means many funded PhD positions. US universities also train a huge number of international PhD students: they earn about 38% of US science and engineering doctorates. A US degree is respected everywhere.

Can I apply? (Eligibility)

Many people ask me, “Am I good enough to apply?” In most cases, yes. Many students with fewer qualifications get funded PhDs simply because they started. Here is what actually matters.

The minimum requirements

Every university lists its minimum requirements on its graduate admissions website. They are usually:

  • a degree related to the PhD program,
  • a minimum grade, often a GPA of about 3.0 out of 4.0 (each university explains how it converts Indian percentages or CGPA),
  • an English test score (TOEFL, IELTS or similar).

If you meet these, you can apply. Check the requirements for each university and program you are interested in.

If you have a 4-year bachelor’s degree

You can apply for a PhD directly. In the US, many departments accept PhD students straight after a 4-year bachelor’s degree; you don’t need a master’s first. BSc Agriculture, BTech and BE in India are 4-year degrees, so they usually meet this requirement. Some departments still prefer or ask for a master’s, so check each program. With only a bachelor’s, it is usually easier to get a funded master’s position than a PhD, so you can try for both.

If you have a 3-year bachelor’s degree (BSc, BA, BCom)

Most US graduate schools ask for a 4-year bachelor’s degree or its equivalent, and they usually don’t count a 3-year degree from India as equivalent on its own. In that case you need a master’s degree after your 3-year bachelor’s (3 + 2 years) before you apply for a PhD. A few universities accept some 3-year degrees under conditions, for example a first-class degree from a highly rated (NAAC-accredited) college, but this is mostly for master’s programs. Always check the “international applicants” or “India” section of the university’s graduate admissions page.

If you want to change your field

You can’t choose any subject at random. You need some background related to the field. But PhD research is interdisciplinary, which means it combines knowledge from different subjects. For example, agricultural research needs engineers, computer scientists and data experts, and biology departments take students from biotechnology, agriculture, zoology and more. If you want to move to a new field, show the professor how your skills can help their research.

If your grades are not very high

You need to meet the university’s minimum grade. Above that minimum, your CGPA does not decide much. Professors care more about your research skills and your motivation.

If you have a study gap or work experience

A gap after your studies or a few years of work does not stop you. I worked for two years after my MSc. Your work experience can even help, if you show what you learned and how it is useful for the research.

If you don’t have publications

Publications help, but they are not required. Many students start a PhD with no papers. Step 4 explains this in detail.

If you are applying from outside the US (for example from India)

You don’t need to be in the US to apply. Some professors may prefer students who are already in the US, because they can start sooner and don’t need a visa, but most PhD positions are open to students from any country. Remember: you only need one professor to say yes.

If you are still studying

Use this time well. Read advertised PhD positions to see which skills labs want, and learn the ones you are missing. Try to get research experience through an internship or a project at a research institute.

  1. Get your English test done first

    Start with the English test. Almost every US university asks international students for an English test score, and it was the only money I spent before I had a PhD position.

    Why take it before contacting professors

    When a professor likes your profile, they often want to move quickly. In the interview they may ask, “Do you meet the English requirement for admission?” If you answer “I still have to take the test,” they may choose another candidate who already has everything ready. Some professors need a student to start as soon as possible.

    Which test to take

    TOEFL and IELTS are accepted by almost all US universities. Some also accept the Duolingo English Test. Check the admissions page of the universities you are interested in.

    What score you need

    For admission, you only need the minimum score the university asks for, for example 6.5 in IELTS. A higher score does not increase your chances of admission. It is just a basic requirement you need to pass.

    If you want to work as a teaching assistant

    Here the speaking score matters more. Many universities ask teaching assistants for a higher speaking score than they ask for admission, for example around 26 out of 30 in TOEFL speaking (TOEFL changed to a new 1–6 scale in January 2026, where it is about 5.5) or around 8 in IELTS speaking. If your score is lower, they usually give you their own speaking test or an English course after you arrive. So if your funding may come from a teaching assistantship, try to get a good speaking score.

    When you may not need the test

    Many US universities don’t ask for an English test if your degree is from a country on their exemption list, usually countries like the US, UK, Ireland, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. India is usually not on these lists, even if your degree was taught in English, though a few universities accept a waiver request with a letter confirming English-medium instruction. Check each university’s graduate admissions page.

    The GRE

    The GRE is a separate general exam. In agriculture, entomology and biology, most PhD programs don’t require it anymore. For example, entomology programs at Cornell, NC State, Wisconsin and Iowa State don’t require it, and Cornell doesn’t even use it. My program doesn’t require it either. But it depends on the university: some programs still say “recommended,” and a few still require it. It is also more common in fields like mathematics, economics and some engineering and IT programs.

    So check the admission page of each program on your list. If none of them require it, don’t spend time and money on it. If a program says “optional,” you can apply without it.

    Plan for score delivery: official scores usually arrive within a few days to two weeks, depending on the test, and then have to be sent to each university.

    What I did

    I took TOEFL just before I started applying. I learned the exam format from YouTube videos. For listening and speaking, I watched Friends, downloaded the episode transcripts and read them out loud to get used to how people talk in a flow. My score came about 30 days after the test.

  2. Understand the ways to apply

    Many people think there is only one way to get a PhD: apply to universities. There are actually three ways.

    1

    Apply to the university directly

    You submit an application with all documents on the university’s website and go through the admission process. If you are admitted, funding is decided after that: it may come from a professor, from a teaching assistant position, or sometimes there is none. With this way you usually apply to many universities and pay an application fee for each one.

    2

    Contact a professor first

    You email professors whose research interests you. If a professor wants to take you as their PhD student, you discuss funding and details with them first. Then the professor asks you to apply to their university, and you complete the official application. With this way you usually apply to only one university.

    3

    Through connections

    Some professors don’t advertise positions. Instead, they ask people they know, often at international research organisations, to recommend good students. If you do an internship or a short job at such an organisation, you can build these connections.

    Which way to choose

    You can use more than one. When universities are accepting applications (usually a few months each year), apply to universities, because many professors in each department can see your application. The rest of the year, email professors for the next round.

    Why using both ways helps

    • In some departments, professors can only choose students from the applications the department has already shortlisted. In others, professors who have their own grants can choose students directly. From outside, you can’t always tell which one it is.
    • Teaching assistant positions are paid by the university, so applying to the university can get you funding even when no professor has grant money. Research assistant positions are paid by professors, so for those it is better to contact professors directly.

    What I did

    I didn’t use a consultancy. I did everything myself, using LinkedIn and AI tools to find openings and professors. I mostly contacted professors and applied to only one university, after a professor selected me.

  3. Find universities and professors

    Finding a funded PhD is mostly about information, not only qualifications. You can’t apply for a position you never heard about.

    Make a list of universities

    • Start with research universities. Search for R1 universities (very high research activity). If your field is agriculture, also search for land-grant universities that have your department. These usually have more research projects and more funding. This does not guarantee funding, but it is a good place to start.
    • Use AI to build the list. Ask an AI tool which universities offer a PhD in your subject or do research in your area.
    • Go to each department page. On each university’s website, open your department’s page. You will find the list of professors (often called “faculty”).
    • Don’t only look at top-ranked universities. Choose universities where professors work on your kind of research. In the end, one professor has to take you.

    Choose the right professors

    • Look for professors who do a lot of research. Professors divide their time between research, teaching and extension (sharing knowledge with farmers and the public). Professors who focus on research get more grants, and grants pay PhD students.
    • Visit their lab website. See how many PhD students and postdocs work there. A busy lab usually means active research and funding. Many lab pages also say clearly whether the professor is looking for new students. This tells you whom to email and whom not to.
    • Your research doesn’t need to match exactly. A PhD is training. What matters is that your skills are useful for the professor’s projects, and that they can train you without too much effort. Ask yourself: why should this professor choose me?
    • Junior and senior professors are different. A newer (assistant) professor usually has more time for you but may expect more work. A senior professor may have less time, so you work more independently.

    Use LinkedIn

    If you are looking for a PhD in the USA, LinkedIn can become one of your best sources of openings, if you set it up the right way.

    • Follow and connect only with your field. Professors, PhD students, postdocs, labs and university departments in the US that work on what you want to do. Try to reach at least 100 such connections.
    • LinkedIn then works for you. It starts suggesting more people from your field automatically, and your feed fills with their posts.
    • You see openings early. Many professors post their funded PhD positions on LinkedIn, and others repost them, so they reach you quickly.
    • Follow pages that post positions regularly. Some pages share funded PhD, master’s and postdoc openings almost every day. Here are a few I follow:

    What I did

    I followed and connected with professors, researchers, labs and PhD students in my field. After a few weeks, my feed was full of funded openings, and I also scrolled back through the last month of posts. The position I finally got was one I found on LinkedIn. I also read the advertised positions and noted the skills they asked for, so I knew what to learn while I was searching.

  4. Prepare a clean CV

    Your email gets the professor’s attention. Your CV gives them the details. A clean CV makes it easy for them to see what you can do.

    How to write the CV

    • Keep it short: two pages at most. Make it easy to read quickly. Use tables where they help, and avoid big empty spaces.
    • Only what you already have: education, research experience, skills, publications, presentations and your English test score. Your research interests and plans go in your email, not the CV.
    • Skip the obvious: don’t describe things every student in your degree does, like regular coursework. One line is enough.
    • Only write what you can explain: in the interview, professors can ask about anything on your CV.

    Publications are not compulsory

    This is the question I get most often. Publications are good to have, but they are not required. Many of my friends came straight after their master’s with no papers and still got funded PhDs. US programs mostly look for a motivated student with the right basic skills.

    • Paper submitted but not published yet? You can still list it on your CV: write the title, the journal name and “(Under review)”.
    • How many papers you need: one paper from your own research is enough to show you know how research writing works. Having many papers doesn’t matter much.
    • Paper delayed? If someone asks, just explain honestly, for example that your supervisor was away and it is now with them for review.

  5. Email professors the right way

    Emailing professors is the most important part of the whole process.

    When to start emailing

    • About 6 to 12 months before you want to start. For a PhD starting in August (Fall), January is a good time to begin. It takes time to learn how to write good emails and to contact enough professors.
    • Don’t wait for a perfect CV. If you will add something important soon, like a paper or a new skill, wait for it. Otherwise, start now.
    • Don’t wait for advertisements. Many professors only mention openings on their lab website, and once a position is posted on LinkedIn, many people apply.

    What to write in the email

    • Write a new email for each professor. Don’t send the same email to everyone and only change the name. (I made this mistake when I started.)
    • Introduce yourself in one line.
    • Write about their research. Read their recent papers, find one specific part that really interests you, and explain why. This is what makes your email different from hundreds of others.
    • Say what you want: that you are interested in doing a PhD in their lab, and that your CV is attached.
    • Keep it short: one or two small paragraphs, so they read it right away instead of saving it for later and forgetting.
    • Don’t ask about funding in the first email. If they are interested in you, funding will be discussed in the interview or later.
    • Don’t send your own full research plan. In a PhD, professors help you build your research question within their area.
    • Attach only your CV. You don’t need an SOP, recommendation letters or a proposal at this stage; they will ask if they need more. Mention your English test score inside your CV.

    If you are emailing about an advertised position

    Follow exactly what the advertisement asks for. It also helps to first ask one or two genuine questions about the project, so you stand out.

    If you are applying for a master’s or a postdoc

    For a master’s position, writing about the professor’s work matters even more, because you may not have much research experience yet. For a postdoc, your previous research must match the professor’s research much more closely.

    How many emails, and following up

    • Expect to send many emails. Professors may get hundreds of emails for one position. You might find the right professor after 50 emails or after 500. (I emailed about 60 in my first month.)
    • No reply after 2 to 3 weeks? Send one short, polite follow-up email.
    • Try again later. A professor’s funding situation changes, so contact the same professors again every few months or each semester.
    • Connect on LinkedIn too. Ask genuine questions about their papers. Professors enjoy talking about their work, and they will remember you.

  6. Understand professor replies

    Many people get confused by professors’ replies. Here are the common replies, what they usually mean, and what you can do next.

    Reading between the lines: professors in the US are usually very polite, so even a no can sound nice. Signs of real interest: they ask you questions, ask for documents, suggest a call, or give a timeline. Signs of a polite no: general praise, no next step, and “best of luck with your search.”

    “Thank you for your interest. Please apply through the university.”

    What it usually means

    Often a standard reply. Some professors can only choose students from the applications the department shortlists, and some don’t want to handle hundreds of emails. It does not mean your profile is weak, but it is not a promise either.

    What you can do

    If the program suits you, apply. You can ask: “Should I mention your name in my application?” If the professor has already talked to you or interviewed you and then says apply, that is a very good sign.

    “Please apply and mention my name in your application.”

    What it usually means

    Better than the standard reply. The professor wants to see your application when it reaches the department.

    What you can do

    Apply before the deadline, mention their name in your statement, and send a short email after you submit so they can look for it.

    “I really like your profile, but I don’t have funding right now.”

    What it usually means

    This can mean two things. Case 1: they really have no money at the moment and would consider you later. Case 2: it is a polite no. People in the US are usually very polite, and a nice reply is not always a yes. You can often tell the difference: if they give details (when they expect a grant, another professor to contact, a TA option, “stay in touch”), it is usually real. If the reply is short and general and ends with “best of luck,” it is usually a polite no.

    What you can do

    Ask one clear question: “If funding becomes available, would you consider me? When would be a good time to check back?” You can also ask whether a teaching assistantship is possible in the department. If they don’t answer that, move on.

    “I don’t have funding right now” or “I have no space in my lab.”

    What it usually means

    This is about timing, not about you. Professors take new students only when they get a new grant or when a current student finishes. A lab may hire two or three students and then nobody for a year or two. Right now this reply is more common than before: since 2025, US federal research funding has been cut and uncertain, many grants were frozen or terminated, and top research universities admitted about 15% fewer PhD students for 2026.

    What you can do

    Thank them, stay in touch, and contact them again after three to six months.

    “I am expecting a grant. Funding may be available later this year.”

    What it usually means

    A real possibility, but not a promise. Grants can be delayed or not funded.

    What you can do

    Ask when they expect to know, and follow up around that time. Keep contacting other professors in the meantime.

    “You may be a good fit for Dr. X’s lab.”

    What it usually means

    A referral. Usually it means they can’t take you, but they think your profile is good.

    What you can do

    Email Dr. X and mention who referred you in the first line. Thank the first professor.

    “Can you send your transcripts, writing sample or references?”

    What it usually means

    They are seriously considering you.

    What you can do

    Send everything quickly and neatly, in one email, with clear file names.

    “Let’s have a Zoom call.”

    What it usually means

    They are seriously considering you. It is not a final yes, but it is a strong sign. After the interview, it depends on how you do (Step 7).

    What you can do

    Reply quickly with your available times, and mention your time zone.

    “Thank you for the interview. I am talking to other candidates and will get back to you.”

    What it usually means

    Normal. They are comparing applicants.

    What you can do

    Wait about two weeks, then send one short, polite follow-up.

    “I will take you as my PhD student. Please apply to the university.”

    What it usually means

    The professor is willing to take you. This is what you want to hear. But the professor cannot give you the official admission; that comes from the university once it checks that you meet the requirements. When a professor has already said yes, the application is usually approved.

    What you can do

    Before or while you apply, ask politely so everything is clear: Which program and term should I apply to? Is the funding a research or teaching assistantship? How much is the stipend, and for how many years? Does it include a tuition waiver and health insurance? Will you inform the department that you support my application? Can I get an application fee waiver? Then apply, and tell the professor when you have submitted.

    “Your background doesn’t match my current projects” or “I am not taking students this year.”

    What it usually means

    A clear no, at least for now.

    What you can do

    Thank them. You can ask if they know a colleague who might be a better fit, and then move on.

    No reply at all.

    What it usually means

    Usually the professor is not taking students right now, the lab is full, or your email got lost in their inbox.

    What you can do

    Send one polite follow-up after two to three weeks. If there is still no reply, move on and try this professor again later.

    If you are not sure what a reply means

    It is completely fine to ask one short, polite question. For example:

    • “Would you be open to a short call to discuss whether I could join your lab?”
    • “Are you planning to take new PhD students for Fall 2027?”
    • “If funding becomes available, would you consider my application?”
    • “Should I apply to the program and mention your name?”

    A clear answer saves you months of waiting. If they don’t reply to a direct question, take that as a no for now and move on.

    What I did

    Most of the professors I emailed in my first month replied that they had no funding or were not taking students. I didn’t take it personally and kept emailing others. My position came from a professor who had an advertised opening.

  7. Prepare for the interview

    Every professor does interviews differently. Some do one interview. Others do several rounds and may ask you to write a short research proposal.

    What they usually ask

    • Your CV. They mostly ask about your research background and the techniques you used. If you list a paper, expect questions like “What exactly did you do in this paper?”
    • Your motivation. Why you want to do a PhD, why this research, why their lab and university, and what you plan to do in the future.
    • Your research, in a presentation. Some professors ask you to present your past research in a few slides.
    • Your documents. They may ask whether you meet the admission requirements, like the English test. Being able to say “yes, I have everything” helps them move forward.

    About your English

    Your English does not have to be perfect. Professors know English is not your first language, and they usually speak slowly and clearly. What matters is that you can explain your point.

    What I did

    I wrote down my answers to the common questions and practised saying them out loud many times, 50 or even 100 times, until the words came naturally and I felt confident. I had three rounds in total: a screening interview, a one-page proposal, and a final interview.

    If you don’t hear back after the interview

    Wait about two weeks, then send one short follow-up email. They may still be interviewing other students.

  8. Apply to the university

    Whether a professor has already said yes or you are applying without a professor, you still need to submit the official application on the university’s website.

    Documents you need

    • transcripts (mark sheets) and degree certificates,
    • English test score,
    • CV,
    • statement of purpose (SOP),
    • usually three letters of recommendation (LORs).

    Writing your SOP

    Don’t copy a template, because another student might send the same one to the same professor. Read a few examples only to understand the structure. Then write your own story: what made you interested in this field, why you want to do this research, and what your goal is. You can use AI to correct the language.

    Choosing who writes your recommendation letters

    Ask people who supervised your work closely, like your master’s thesis advisor or your manager at work. If you studied in India, letters from your Indian teachers and supervisors are completely fine.

    Application fee and fee waivers

    The application fee is usually around $50–125 per program, and international applicants sometimes pay a little more. Some universities offer fee waivers (permission to apply without paying), but the rules differ, and some waivers are only for US applicants. Check the university’s fee waiver policy first; if nothing is listed, you can politely email the department and ask. Some of my friends asked and got it.

    Deadlines

    Each university sets its own deadlines; search for “graduate application deadline” on their website. For a PhD starting in August (Fall), most deadlines are between December and February. For a January start (Spring), deadlines are often around September–October, and not every program admits in Spring.

    When you get a decision

    Universities usually decide after the deadline, once they have received all applications. Professors and advertised positions usually decide whenever they find the right student; this is called “rolling basis.”

    If you get admission without funding

    Be careful here. Admission without funding means you pay the tuition (international tuition is high) and your living costs yourself. To get your I-20, the university will ask you to show proof that you can pay the full first-year cost.

    Some people say “go first, you will get an assistantship after the first semester.” That sometimes happens for master’s students, but it is not guaranteed, and for a PhD of 4 to 6 years it is a big risk. Before you decide, ask the professor or department directly whether any funding is possible. If not, it is usually better to keep applying for funded positions.

    How many universities to apply to

    The more places you apply, the higher your chances. You can apply to several universities, get admission and an I-20 from more than one, and choose one at the end.

    I started a PhD in India before coming here and left it after one year, because I wanted to do better research. If you are in a similar situation, describe yourself with your master’s degree and research experience. Wanting better research and better opportunities is a reason everyone understands.

  9. Understand your funding and costs

    I used to think studying in the US would need a huge amount of money. A PhD is completely different.

    $0tuition: it is waived in most funded programs
    $20–35ktypical stipend per year at most universities
    Coveredhealth insurance in most cases, and research costs from the project

    Why you are paid

    As a PhD student you work part-time as a research assistant (paid from a professor’s grant) or a teaching assistant (paid by the department). You also take classes and do your own research.

    Most PhDs are funded

    It is very hard to pay for several years yourself, so most US PhDs in science come with funding. If a professor selects you, they usually make sure there is money for you. That is why getting the position is the main goal. Funding is tighter now than a few years ago, so expect more competition.

    Is the stipend paid all year?

    Research assistant positions are often paid for all 12 months. Teaching assistant positions may be paid for 9 months, because there are fewer classes in summer, but many students find summer work in a lab.

    How long the funding lasts

    Funding usually lasts for the length of your program. My project is funded for 3 years; some of my friends have 5. Unlike in India, you can’t easily extend a PhD: after the funded period ends, you would have to pay yourself.

    How much is the stipend?

    At most universities, a PhD stipend is roughly $20,000 to $35,000 a year. A few top private universities and universities in expensive cities pay more, around $40,000 to $50,000. Here are some published examples:

    University / sourceYearly stipend

    Amounts change every year. Always check the current numbers on the department’s website or in your offer letter.

    What decides how far your stipend goes

    A bigger stipend is not always better. What matters is the stipend compared with the cost of living where you will live.

    • City and state: rent is the biggest expense. A $25,000 stipend in a small college town can go further than $40,000 in a big, expensive city.
    • 9 or 12 months: check whether the stipend is paid all year or only during the semesters.
    • Taxes: the stipend is taxable income in the US, so what you receive is a bit lower than the offer.
    • Fees and insurance: some universities don’t cover all fees or the full health insurance cost. Ask what you still have to pay.
    • Your lifestyle: sharing an apartment, cooking at home and using a shared or used car make a big difference.

    Can you save money?

    Yes. Research costs and conferences are usually paid from the project funds, not from your stipend, so the stipend is only for your living costs.

    What I see

    From my own experience and what I see with my friends, with a normal lifestyle and shared housing, most of us save a good amount, around $500 to $1,000 a month depending on the city. In Indian rupees (about ₹95 per dollar in 2026), that is roughly ₹47,000 to ₹95,000 every month. Many students are able to send good money home, while doing a PhD that costs them nothing in tuition.

    Part-time jobs

    On an F-1 visa, you can’t work off campus without special authorization. On campus, you can work up to 20 hours a week while classes are in session, and a research or teaching assistantship usually counts as those 20 hours. So your assistantship already is your part-time job.

    What I actually spent

    StageCost
    English test (TOEFL), before any positionabout ₹20,000 (around $200)
    University application, after the professor accepted meabout ₹7,000
    Whole visa processabout ₹70,000 (around $700–800)

    That was all. No tuition, no loan. For reference, the official government fees now are a $185 visa application fee and a $350 SEVIS fee, plus a new $250 visa integrity fee created by law in 2025 that is being rolled out. Check the current amounts before you apply.

  10. I-20 and visa

    After you are admitted, the university sends you an I-20. You need it to apply for your F-1 student visa.

    Do I need to show money in my bank account?

    The I-20 shows two numbers: the estimated cost of one year (tuition plus living costs) and the funding the university is giving you.

    • If your funding is equal to or more than the estimated cost, you don’t need to show any extra money.
    • If your funding is less, for example a 9-month stipend against a 12-month cost, you only need to show bank funds for the difference.

    The visa interview

    Officers check whether you can pay for your studies. A funded PhD offer answers that question clearly, because the university’s support is written on your I-20.

    What changed recently

    The student visa process has become stricter since 2025. Applicants now attend an in-person interview, must list their social media accounts from the last five years on the DS-160 form and keep them public, and appointment waits in India have been long. Student visa issuances to Indians fell sharply in 2025. So book your interview as soon as you receive your I-20, and keep your documents complete and consistent.

    Positions for “US citizens or permanent residents only”

    Some positions say this because their money comes from a source that can only pay US citizens or permanent residents, such as certain federal fellowships and training grants. Read the eligibility line before you apply. Most research assistantships paid from a professor’s grant are open to international students.

    Changing universities after getting your visa

    If you already have a visa based on one university’s I-20 and want to move to another, talk to the international student office first. Student status rules have been changing: a new DHS rule ending “duration of status” was published in July 2026 but was blocked by a court before it took effect, so the current rules still apply while the case continues.

    Visa rules and fees change. Always check the official US embassy website for your country.

Life during and after the PhD

A normal day

My research is in the lab, so I work about 8 am to 5 pm. Other students set their own hours. A PhD includes classes, lab or field work, extension work, presentations, writing papers and attending conferences.

Conferences

PhD students usually present their research at conferences every year. The costs are often paid from the project funding.

Qualifying exams

Most programs have an exam partway through the PhD to check that you are ready to continue (called a qualifying or comprehensive exam). The format depends on your program and professor: some ask you to write and present a research proposal, others have an oral exam. If you take your classes and research seriously, you will pass.

Feeling lonely

It can feel lonely sometimes, especially at first. When you feel you are learning and growing, it gets easier, and finding a good group of friends helps a lot.

After the PhD

After graduating, F-1 students can get 12 months of OPT, and graduates in STEM fields can apply for a 24-month extension, so up to 3 years in total. Try to have a job lined up before you finish. Note that in October 2026 the government proposed new fees on OPT; they are not in effect, but check the latest rules when you get close to graduating. If you want to work in industry, internships and networking during your PhD make a big difference.

My own path

I did a BSc in Agriculture and an MSc (Ag) in Entomology in India, then worked for two years on research projects. I started a PhD in India but left after a year because I wanted to do better research. I applied on my own, without a consultancy.

  1. Month 0Took TOEFL. Got my score about 30 days later.
  2. Month 1Emailed about 60 professors who work on insects using molecular and bioinformatics methods. Most replied that they had no funding or were not taking students.
  3. Month 2Found an advertised position on LinkedIn, emailed the professor, and got my first interview about 20 days later.
  4. Month 3Was shortlisted, wrote a one-page proposal, had a final interview, and was selected.
  5. After thatApplied to the university, received admission and my I-20, then got my visa.

Looking back, I only contacted professors I chose and never applied to universities directly. That limited my chances, because other professors who might have liked my profile never saw it. If I did it again, I would do both.

This guide is based on my personal experience in an agriculture-related PhD in the US. Requirements, funding and costs vary by university, program and year. Always check the official university and embassy websites.
