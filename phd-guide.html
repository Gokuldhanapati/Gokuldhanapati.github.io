How to get a fully funded PhD in the USA

The complete process I followed, from finding a professor to getting the visa, in ten steps. Have a question? Ask it at the end of the page and I will answer there for everyone.

  1. Start early and plan the timeline

    A funded PhD application usually takes 12 to 18 months from first research to arriving in the US. Most programs admit students for Fall (August) and some also for Spring (January).

    • 12–18 months before: decide your research area, shortlist universities and professors, start test preparation.
    • 9–12 months before: email professors, take tests, ask for recommendation letters.
    • Application deadlines: for Fall, many programs close between December and February. Funding is often decided from the earliest deadline, so do not wait for the last one.
    • After applying: interviews, offers, then I-20 and visa.

    Every program sets its own deadlines and requirements. Always confirm on the official department page.

  2. Understand how PhD funding works

    In the US, a “fully funded” PhD usually means the university pays your tuition and gives you a monthly stipend to live on. In return you work as a research or teaching assistant.

    • Graduate Research Assistantship (GRA): paid from a professor's research grant. You work on their project, which usually becomes your dissertation.
    • Graduate Teaching Assistantship (GTA): paid by the department. You help teach labs or courses.
    • Fellowships: awarded by the university or outside organisations, often with no work requirement.

    In fields like entomology and agriculture, most funding comes from a professor's grant. That is why contacting professors before you apply matters so much.

  3. Find professors and labs

    Start from the research, not the university ranking. Look for professors whose recent work matches what you want to do.

    • Search Google Scholar for recent papers in your topic and note the authors and their universities.
    • Read department faculty pages and lab websites. Look for “Join the lab” or “Open positions”.
    • Check job boards and mailing lists where funded positions are posted.
    • Keep a simple spreadsheet: professor, university, research area, funding status, deadline, date emailed, reply.

  4. Email professors

    A short, specific email works better than a long one. The professor should understand in 30 seconds who you are and why their lab.

    • Subject: something clear, e.g. “Prospective PhD student, Fall 2027: honey bee virology”.
    • Paragraph 1: who you are, your degree and research background.
    • Paragraph 2: which of their papers or projects interests you, and how your experience fits.
    • Paragraph 3: ask whether they are accepting funded students for the coming term.
    • Attach your CV as a PDF. Do not attach many files.
    • If there is no reply after about two weeks, send one polite follow-up.

    Write each email for that professor. Generic emails sent to many people are easy to spot and rarely answered.

  5. Prepare your documents

    • CV: education, research experience, publications, presentations, skills. Keep it clean, 2–3 pages.
    • Statement of Purpose (SOP): your research interests, what you have done, and why this program and professor. Tailor it for each application.
    • Letters of recommendation: usually three, ideally from people who supervised your research. Ask at least a month before the deadline and send them your CV and SOP.
    • Transcripts and degree certificates: some universities need a credential evaluation (for example WES). Check early, it can take weeks.
    • Publications: keep links ready. They are not required, but they help.

  6. Take the required tests

    • English proficiency: most universities accept TOEFL iBT or IELTS; some also accept Duolingo. Check the minimum score for both the graduate school and the department.
    • GRE: many programs no longer require it. Check each program before spending money on it.

    Send official scores to each university from the testing service. This can take a couple of weeks, so do it before the deadline.

  7. Submit the applications

    Apply through the graduate school's online portal. You will upload your documents, enter your recommenders' emails (they receive a link to upload their letters), and pay an application fee.

    • Mention the professor you have been talking to in your application and SOP.
    • Some programs offer application fee waivers. Ask the department if you need one.
    • After submitting, tell the professor you have applied.

  8. Prepare for interviews

    Interviews are usually on Zoom with the professor, and sometimes with lab members or a committee.

    • Be ready to explain your past research clearly in a few minutes.
    • Read the professor's recent papers and connect them to your interests.
    • Prepare your own questions: the project, funding duration, lab culture, mentoring style.

  9. Read the offer letter

    Before accepting, check the offer letter carefully:

    • Monthly or yearly stipend amount, and for how many years it is guaranteed.
    • Tuition waiver: full or partial.
    • Health insurance and any fees you still have to pay.
    • Whether funding is GRA, GTA or fellowship, and the expected work hours.

    It is fine to ask the professor or department to clarify anything before you sign.

  10. I-20 and F-1 visa

    After you accept, the university issues an I-20. You will usually need to show proof of funding (your offer letter) and a copy of your passport.

    • Pay the SEVIS fee and fill in the DS-160 visa application form.
    • Book your F-1 visa interview at the US embassy or consulate. Slots can be limited, so book early.
    • Carry your I-20, offer letter, passport, DS-160 confirmation, SEVIS receipt, transcripts and test scores to the interview.
    • Be ready to explain your research plan and your funding in simple words.

    Visa rules and fees change. Check the official US embassy website for your country before you apply.
